\subsection{Setup}
\label{app:setup}

\paragraph{Splits.}
The split is a date-ordered $13{,}815/3{,}944/3{,}352$ partition of the
$21{,}111$ idea-level samples in \textsc{IdeaDelta}.

\paragraph{Configuration.}
All runs share one configuration and vary a single parameter at a time:
\texttt{deberta-v3-base} backbone \citep{he2023deberta}, 8 epochs, batch size
2, learning rate
$1.5\times10^{-5}$ with an encoder learning-rate ratio of $0.2$, eight semantic
units with slot competition enabled, and a fixed joint-prediction checkpoint
supplying the encoder initialisation. The checkpoint reported in the main text
is selected on dev; test is traversed once, at final evaluation, and is not used
to select a checkpoint.

\paragraph{Dense retrieval baseline.}
The dense-RAG rows of Table~\ref{tab:panel-a} replace the stored candidate
lists with retrieval performed at query time \citep{karpukhin2020dpr}. We
embed every canonical idea
with \texttt{bge-large-en-v1.5} \citep{xiao2024cpack}, index the ideas whose
date precedes the
target's, query the index with the target text, and pass the top five hits to
the same single-call prompt the \emph{direct} rows use. Nothing else changes:
the same backbones, the same prompt, the same labels and the same scoring
script.
Each domain is indexed separately, so a target retrieves within its own domain
as every other row does. Inside that index the date is the only constraint
applied: the search ranges over all aspects, whereas the stored candidate lists
come from aspect-restricted nearest-neighbour recall together with the
cross-type pass of \S\ref{sec:dataset}.

\paragraph{Metrics.}
We use four evaluation metrics, computed over the four
ordered grades \textsc{low}, \textsc{weak}, \textsc{moderate}, \textsc{high},
indexed $0$ to $3$. \textbf{Accuracy} is the fraction of ideas whose predicted
grade equals the gold grade. It weights every example equally and does not
distinguish errors by grade distance, so frequent classes can dominate it.
\textbf{Macro-F1} averages the per-class $F_1$ over the four grades with
equal weight, which charges a system for abandoning a small class rather than
letting the large ones absorb the loss. \textbf{QWK} is quadratic weighted
kappa \citep{cohen1968weighted},
\begin{equation*}
 \kappa_w = 1 - \frac{\sum_{i,j} w_{ij} O_{ij}}{\sum_{i,j} w_{ij} E_{ij}},
 \qquad w_{ij} = (i-j)^2/9,
\end{equation*}
where $O$ is the confusion matrix and $E$ the matrix expected from the two
marginals if prediction and gold were independent. QWK measures
chance-corrected ordinal agreement, penalizing distant grade errors more
heavily than near misses. With a nonzero denominator, it equals $1$ for exact
agreement, $0$ when observed weighted disagreement matches the chance
reference, and is negative when disagreement exceeds that reference.
A constant predictor has QWK $0$ on our evaluation sets; independent random
predictions need not yield exactly $0$ in a finite sample. QWK depends on the
assigned grades and their marginal distributions, so it does not isolate
ranking quality from grade calibration.
\textbf{MAE} (mean absolute error) measures the average absolute difference
between predicted and gold novelty grades:
\begin{equation*}
 \mathrm{MAE}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\widehat y_i-y_i|,
\end{equation*}
where $\widehat y_i$ and $y_i$ are the predicted and gold grade indices in
$\{0,1,2,3\}$, respectively, and $N$ is the number of evaluated ideas.
Lower values indicate smaller grade errors. Among constant predictions, a median of the
gold-label distribution minimizes MAE; this does not characterize the best
input-dependent predictor. We therefore interpret MAE alongside the other
metrics. Every table in the paper reports these four. On RINoBench the expert
1--5 scale is folded to the same four levels before any of them is computed, so
every number in Table~\ref{tab:panel-b} is on the scale described here.

\paragraph{Per-domain metrics.}
Per-domain figures are recomputed from the stored predictions rather than read
from the trainer, and reconcile with the trainer's own pooled figures to within
$5\times10^{-5}$ on all four metrics.

\subsection{Parameter sensitivity}
\label{app:sensitivity}

Each sweep varies one parameter from the default configuration of
Table~\ref{tab:panel-a} and holds the rest fixed: the upstream pair and joint
products, the encoder initialisation, the evaluation files and the seed. Every
point is a single run at seed 173, except for the seed sweep below.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{pic/param_sensitivity/topk.png}
\caption{Sensitivity to the evidence budget $K$ in the three domains. The
default is $K{=}5$, the checkpoint of Table~\ref{tab:panel-a}. $K$ specifies
the number of input slots, including padding when fewer priors are available.}
\label{fig:topk}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Evidence budget: gains flatten beyond four input slots.}
Figure~\ref{fig:topk} varies the input-slot budget $K$
over $K=2,3,4,5,6,8$. Accuracy rises steeply from $K{=}2$ to $K{=}4$ ($0.731 \to
0.806$ on Graph ML, $0.716 \to 0.772$ on computer vision, $0.726 \to 0.789$ on
time series) and then flattens across the three domains. The configured slot
budget does not by itself establish how many non-padding priors a target
receives. These curves show sensitivity to $K$ under the evaluated setup;
they do not distinguish limited evidence availability from limited benefit
of additional evidence.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{pic/param_sensitivity/train_frac.png}
\caption{Sensitivity to the amount of EGRD training data in the three domains.
Development and test sets stay fixed, and $100\%$ is the default.}
\label{fig:train-frac}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Training-set size: gains differ across domains.}
Figure~\ref{fig:train-frac} varies the number of training ideas over $25\%$,
$50\%$, $75\%$ and $100\%$ of the training split, drawn as nested stratified prefixes so that
consecutive points add samples without reordering what came before. Macro-F1
rises steadily with the fraction on Graph ML ($0.7627 \to 0.8010$) and time
series ($0.7573 \to 0.7760$). Computer vision does not improve consistently
beyond $50\%$ and ends
at $0.7669$ against a peak of $0.7788$, a gap of $0.012$. More training data
helps in most of the domains we measure, and the spread across the four
fractions stays small: Accuracy moves by at most $0.039$ in any domain.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{pic/param_sensitivity/max_length.png}
\caption{Sensitivity to encoder input length on the three domain test splits.
The default is 384, and every setting scores the same test ideas and labels.}
\label{fig:maxlen}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Encoder input length: limited sensitivity over the tested range.}
Figure~\ref{fig:maxlen} varies the encoder input length over 128, 192, 256 and
384 tokens. The four lengths separate the domains by little: no domain moves monotonically
with input length, and tripling the input moves Macro-F1 by at most $0.017$ in
any domain. QWK spans $0.0076$ on
Graph ML, $0.0023$ on computer vision and $0.0062$ on time series across the
four lengths, indicating that ordinal agreement changes little over the tested
input lengths. These results show limited performance sensitivity from 128
to 384 tokens; they do not establish how much of each input is retained at
128 tokens.

\paragraph{Seed stability: the reported model is not a selected seed.}
Table~\ref{tab:seed-noise} varies nothing but the random seed, running the
default configuration on seeds 173, 174 and 175 with identical data and code and
reporting the per-domain sample standard deviations. The spread is $0.004$--$0.007$ in Accuracy, $0.002$--$0.005$ in Macro-F1 and
$0.003$--$0.007$ in MAE, an order of magnitude below the ablation gaps of
Table~\ref{tab:ablation}, so
the headline numbers describe the configuration rather than one fortunate run,
and the three sweeps above should be read by the shape of a curve rather than by
the difference between adjacent points.

\subsection{Case-study ideas}
\label{app:case-study}

\paragraph{Construction of Figure~\ref{fig:case-study}.}
Panel (a) is read off the forward pass of the trained model and fits nothing.
For the other two panels the test ideas are split $80/20$ with the three cases
held out, and every supervised step is fit on the $80\%$ part alone. Panel (b)
standardises $\boldsymbol{z}_R$ on that part, ranks its coordinates by
univariate $F$-statistic against the grade, and shows the $140$ that separate it
best. Panel (c) reduces $\boldsymbol{z}_R$ to $50$ principal components and
projects it onto the first two axes of a shrinkage linear discriminant fit on
the four grades. The probe reported in the panel is separate from both: a
five-fold cross-validated multinomial logistic regression on standardised
$\boldsymbol{z}_R$, scored out of fold.
