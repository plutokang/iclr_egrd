\subsection{Dataset construction}
\label{app:dataset}

\paragraph{Sources and idea extraction.}
Papers are drawn from arXiv in three areas---graph machine learning, computer
vision, and time series. Selection prefers work that was subsequently published
and, among that, papers at major venues. From each paper's title, abstract and
introduction an LLM extracts
innovation claims and assigns each an innovation aspect; a second LLM pass
discards claims that are not innovation claims at all---open-source and
reproducibility statements, bare state-of-the-art assertions, and engineering
deliverables---so that later stages judge substantive claims rather than
boilerplate.

\paragraph{Candidate priors.}
Idea texts are embedded and indexed for top-$K$ nearest-neighbour recall, and
candidate relations are formed under a hybrid rule: high similarity is accepted
directly, low similarity is rejected directly, and the uncertain band is passed
to an LLM. The base build pairs ideas of the same type only, which would record
an idea whose closest relative is of another type---a theoretical contribution
nearest to a method---as isolated; a cross-type pass recovers these. At most
$K=5$ ranked priors are retained per target. Pairing stays within a source
domain, and no cross-domain pair is labelled, so the three single-domain subsets
and their union address the same ideas under identifiers namespaced by domain.
The assembled dataset passes its own validation checks with no duplicate pair
keys, no duplicate novelty targets, no invalid pair labels, no target left
without a pair, and no temporal violation.

\paragraph{Annotation.}
All annotation stages use the same judge, \texttt{deepseek-v4-pro}. It labels
pair coverage over the four classes, with the ambiguous band---related but not
covering, partial cover, large cover, and high-similarity pairs the graph left
unconnected---re-judged rather than accepted from the similarity rule. It then
estimates, for each target, the substantive residual that survives the retained
evidence, the sufficiency of that evidence, and the extent to which the claimed
difference is surface-level. Idea-level grades follow by applying
Eq.~(\ref{eq:novelty-rule}) to those quantities. Labels are model-generated;
each stage was reviewed by sampled manual inspection.

\paragraph{Splits and balancing.}
Splits are date-ordered rather than random, so that no training target is dated
after an evaluation target. The cuts fall at 2025-02 and 2025-11, giving
$59{,}269/16{,}436/17{,}171$ pairs and $13{,}815/3{,}944/3{,}352$ ideas. The cut
months are shared at the boundary, so the partition is ordered but not strictly
month-disjoint, and isolation at the level of source papers has not been
established. A balanced training variant resamples the pair training set to
$1{,}000$ examples per coverage class, at most five copies of any example and
marked as duplicates; dev and test keep their natural distribution and are never
resampled. Pair coverage labels are skewed in the natural distribution: of
$92{,}876$ pairs, $41{,}765$ are not covering, $38{,}775$ related but not
covering, $9{,}582$ partial cover, and $2{,}754$ large cover.

\subsection{Validation of Research Novelty Labels}
\label{app:dataset-validity}

Our novelty labels assess the substantive contribution of a target idea beyond
its retrieved prior work. We validate this interpretation through complementary
experiments. First, we examine how well metadata and retrieved prior content
predict the existing labels. We then modify the evidence and repeat annotation
to test whether novelty decreases when the target contribution is already
covered and remains stable when its substantive contribution is unchanged.

\paragraph{Prediction from metadata.}
We first test whether simple metadata can account for the novelty labels
without describing the technical content of prior work. We consider seven
variables: character length, word count, publication year, three research-area
attributes, and the number of retrieved priors. Over all $21{,}111$ ideas, we
measure their individual associations with the novelty grade using Spearman
$\rho$ for continuous variables and $\varepsilon^2$ for categorical variables.
We set the thresholds in advance at $|\rho|\ge0.20$ and
$\varepsilon^2\ge0.06$.

Text length, word count, and publication year show weak associations
($\rho=+0.049$, $+0.058$, and $-0.130$, respectively), while the three
research-area attributes have $\varepsilon^2\le0.033$. All remain below the
corresponding thresholds. The number of priors and their mean coverage are
negatively correlated with novelty ($\rho=-0.286$ and $-0.471$, respectively).
The negative association with coverage is consistent with the intended meaning
of the labels: contributions already covered by prior work receive lower
novelty grades.

Individual associations may overlook information available from several
variables together. We therefore train a classifier on all seven metadata
variables to predict the four novelty grades. We use the date-ordered split in
Section~\ref{sec:dataset-composition}, with $13{,}815$ training ideas and
$3{,}352$ test ideas, and evaluate quadratic weighted kappa (QWK), which accounts
for the ordering of the grades. For comparison, we train the same classifier
using only mean prior coverage. QWK increases from $0.227$ with metadata to
$0.539$ with coverage alone. Coverage thus predicts the labels more accurately
than the metadata considered here, supporting the relevance of the relationship
between the target and prior work.

\paragraph{Prediction with retrieved and random priors.}
We next examine whether the specific retrieved content matters for label
prediction. In the \textsc{true} condition, each target is paired with its five
retrieved priors. In the \textsc{random} condition, these are replaced by five
ideas sampled uniformly from those published before the target. The two
conditions use the same number of priors and total evidence text volume, and
all priors precede the target. We hold the model, hyperparameters, date-ordered
split, and random seeds fixed. Models are trained separately from scratch and
evaluated on the same held-out targets against their original labels.

Across the three seeds, QWK decreases from $0.196$ with retrieved priors to
$0.005\pm0.021$ with random priors. The gap of $0.192$ is about an order of
magnitude larger than the between-seed standard deviation of $0.015$.
Replacing retrieved priors with random earlier work therefore reduces
prediction to near-chance agreement. This comparison supports the importance
of the retrieved content, beyond the amount of evidence supplied.

\paragraph{Response to evidence covering the target contribution.}
The prediction experiments keep the labels fixed. We now test whether the
annotation procedure responds appropriately when the evidence changes.
If an earlier study already contains the target contribution, the assigned
novelty should decrease. We sample $30$ targets across the four grades and
construct one synthetic prior for each target. This prior describes the same
technical contribution and is dated before the target. After adding it to the
evidence set, we repeat the complete annotation procedure: pair coverage
labeling, residual contribution estimation, and grading according to
Eq.~(\ref{eq:novelty-rule}). Synthetic priors have dedicated identifiers and
are marked \texttt{synthetic\_control}. They do not refer to real papers,
authors, or DOIs and are excluded from the released dataset.

Each modified target is compared with five independent annotations of its
original evidence set to account for variation across repeated annotations.
We evaluate recognition of the added prior as large coverage, changes in joint
coverage and residual contribution, and decreases in the novelty score and
grade. Ties are excluded from the exact sign test.

The added prior is labeled large coverage in all $29$ comparable cases.
Joint coverage increases, while residual contribution and novelty score
decrease. The novelty grade decreases for $82.8\%$ of targets
(sign test $p=2.2\times10^{-7}$). These results show that the annotation
procedure lowers novelty when the evidence covers the target contribution.

\paragraph{Stability under changes that preserve the contribution.}
A novelty judgment should also remain stable when the evidence changes without
altering the substantive contribution of the target. For the same $30$ targets,
we reorder the priors, duplicate existing priors, or add unrelated papers.
The novelty grades before and after these changes show high agreement:
$\kappa=0.970$, $0.902$, and $1.000$, respectively. These results complement
the coverage experiment: the judgments respond to evidence that reduces the
remaining contribution and remain comparatively stable under changes that
preserve it.

\paragraph{Evaluation scope.}
The metadata analysis covers $21{,}111$ ideas, and the prediction experiments
use $13{,}815/3{,}944/3{,}352$ training, validation, and test examples ordered
by date. The evidence modification experiments cover $30$ targets, with $150$
baseline repetitions, $30$ coverage comparisons, and $160$ stability
comparisons. Sampling uses a fixed seed and groups ideas by source paper.
Each modified example is compared with its own original version, and
confidence intervals for component-level changes use $2{,}000$ bootstrap
samples clustered by target.
