\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

The evaluation asks three things: whether the decomposition beats prompting a
language model on the same evidence (\S\ref{sec:exp-main}), whether what it
learns survives labels it was not trained on (\S\ref{sec:exp-transfer}), and
what its predictions depend on (\S\ref{sec:exp-ablation},
\S\ref{sec:exp-case}). The last is answerable only because the evidence is
supplied rather than retrieved: on the \textsc{IdeaDelta} test split every
system but one reads the same five admissible priors per target. All rows report
Accuracy, Macro-F1, QWK and MAE; Appendix~\ref{app:setup} gives the splits,
hyperparameters and scoring code.

We compare against a \emph{direct} single call; two multi-stage prompt chains,
\emph{Schopf 2-stage}, the evaluation prompt accompanying RINoBench
\citep{schopf2026rinobench}, and \emph{UKPLab 3-stage}
\citep{afzal2026beyond}; \emph{GraphMind} \citep{dasilva2025graphmind}, which
labels each prior as supporting or contrasting before rating;
\emph{DeepReviewer-7B} \citep{zhu2025deepreview}, a reviewer model fine-tuned on
paper review; and a \emph{dense RAG} group running the \emph{direct} call on
five priors it retrieves itself by dense similarity to the target. All but
DeepReviewer-7B appear once per backbone. Table~\ref{tab:main} reports every
system on the \textsc{IdeaDelta} evaluation set.

The effect of the Schopf chain declines monotonically with how well the backbone
scores without it. Against a single five-prior call, the two-stage chain gains
in all three domains on Qwen3.5, the weakest backbone here, on both Accuracy and
QWK; on deepseek-flash the QWK gain is already gone; and on gpt-5.6-luna, whose
unaided QWK is the highest of the three, it loses in all three domains on both
metrics. That is the pattern to expect if a chain substitutes its own
intermediate reading of the evidence for the one the backbone would produce in a
single pass: worth taking while the backbone's own reading is the weaker of the
two, costly once it is the better. Even where the gain is real it is small, and
Qwen3.5 with the chain still scores below deepseek-flash without one.

Structure alone is not what earns that gain. The three-stage UKPLab chain and
GraphMind fall below the direct call in all nine cells on both metrics, Qwen3.5
included, so a weak backbone gains nothing from being given stages as such ---
only from being given a reading of the evidence better than its own. The
backbone, rather than the chain, sets the level a system reaches: Qwen3.5 is the
weakest of the three under every system in Table~\ref{tab:main}, and no chain
moves it past a stronger backbone. Swapping the backbone under a fixed chain
also moves Accuracy more than swapping the chain under a fixed backbone does.

Replacing the supplied priors with query-time dense retrieval costs ordinal
agreement on every backbone: against the direct call on the same backbone and
domain, Macro-F1 and QWK fall in all nine cells. Accuracy moves the other way on
the weakest backbone, rising on Qwen3.5 while Macro-F1 falls --- the signature
of predictions concentrating on the larger classes, these rows sitting at
$0.318$--$0.372$ Accuracy, at or just above the majority-class band. The group
also compresses the backbone differences Table~\ref{tab:main} otherwise shows,
its Accuracy spanning at most a quarter as much in any domain as the direct
call's. What a system is given to compare against therefore moves the judgment
more than which model or chain performs it. That sharpens the evidence check of
Appendix~\ref{app:dataset-validity}: random priors there reduce label prediction
to near-chance, establishing only that the evidence must be on topic, whereas
dense retrieval is on topic and the gap persists. That persistence shows the
candidate lists carry target--prior relationships generic similarity search does
not recover, which is what the cross-type retrieval pass and the adjudicated
similarity band of \textsc{IdeaDelta} are for.

EGRD leads every baseline in every domain by $0.34$ Accuracy or more over the
best baseline of that domain, a margin that barely varies across the three,
unlike the baselines' ranking among each other. DeepReviewer-7B transfers least:
its ordinal agreement is near zero in all three domains and negative in two, so
fine-tuning on paper review does not recover the grade ordering here.

\subsection{Transfer Evaluation}
\label{sec:exp-transfer}

The comparison above is scored against \textsc{IdeaDelta}'s model-generated
labels. To ask what survives outside that setting, we transfer to human
expertise. RINoBench \citep{schopf2026rinobench} draws ideas from OpenReview
submissions across machine learning as a whole, pairs each with the prior work
admissible for it, and has domain experts rate its novelty from 1 to 5; the
benchmark then measures how closely language models reproduce those ratings.
Its 277 test ideas give a label source independent of ours in both respects
that matter here: the grades are human rather than model-generated, and the
ideas span the field rather than three of its areas. We evaluate the checkpoint
of Table~\ref{tab:main} on them frozen, with no retraining and no fine-tuning,
folding the expert scale to our four grades by merging 4 and 5 and reading the
25 priors the benchmark supplies per target at the trained input width. The
comparison rows are three chains we rerun on the same evidence together with
the systems the benchmark itself reports, all rescored on the folded scale by
the script that produces Table~\ref{tab:main}.
Appendix~\ref{app:transfer} gives the protocol.

EGRD leads both metrics that reward ordering the ideas the way the experts did,
and on QWK no other system reaches two thirds of its value while most of the
table sits near zero. Accuracy and MAE belong to other rows, but reading those
leads as closer agreement mistakes what the folded scale rewards. Merging the
top two expert levels leaves $41.5\%$ of the ideas in a single class, so
answering with that class alone scores $0.4152$ Accuracy and a competitive MAE
while ordering nothing, and the systems that lead those two metrics come close
to doing exactly that: their ordinal agreement is at or near zero, the value a
constant answer earns by construction. Our row reaches the same $0.4152$ from a
prediction spread over all four grades, $16/36/76/149$ against a gold
$15/60/87/115$, and it is that spread the rank-sensitive metrics register.

The agreement is nonetheless partial, and two properties of the
transfer bound it before any model is run. The first is coverage of the
literature. \textsc{IdeaDelta} is built from three fields, whereas RINoBench spans
machine learning as a whole: only 30 of its 277 test ideas fall within
these three fields. The remaining 247 ideas therefore test transfer beyond the
domains covered by our task-specific training data. This shift may limit the
model's ability to parse target units and identify their support in prior work,
making the decomposition less reliable. The second is the
label scale. Transferring a 1--5 expert rating onto four ordered grades requires
merging two of its levels, and the merge is not neutral: it erases the
distinction the experts drew at the top of their scale, and that is exactly
where the mass of the benchmark lies. A model that orders those ideas correctly
has no way left to express the difference, and no metric computed on the folded
scale can credit it. We therefore read the margin as partial agreement between
residual structure learned from model-generated labels and expert judgment,
not as the problem solved.

Because the supplied evidence can be intervened on with the target held fixed,
we can test what the trained model's predictions actually depend on. The three
pipeline interventions cut the chain at successive points.
\textbf{Permuting prior evidence} shuffles the retrieved prior texts across
targets, destroying the semantic content the chain is built on;
\textbf{permuting coverage} shuffles only the joint coverage score and leaves
every prior text in place, destroying what the historical evidence inputs
(\S\ref{sec:method-evidence}) infer from that content; \textbf{zeroing
decomposition representations} sets the unit residual $\boldsymbol{z}_U$,
interaction residual $\boldsymbol{z}_I$ and covered-content representation
$\boldsymbol{z}_C$ to zero, removing these three outputs of the decomposition
(\S\ref{sec:method-decomposition}) together. All three reduce performance on
every metric.
Permuting the prior texts costs the most, dropping Accuracy into the
majority-class band of Table~\ref{tab:main} ($0.312$--$0.356$): without matched
evidence the model scores no better than one that reads nothing. Permuting
coverage keeps every prior text in place and still costs on all four, so what
the historical evidence inputs infer is not recoverable from the texts they read.

Those last two disagree about which metric they hurt. Zeroing misclassifies more
ideas but leaves the ordering close to intact --- QWK $0.7878$ against $0.8958$
for the full model --- its errors falling mostly into adjacent grades, as its
MAE shows. Permuting coverage does the reverse, misclassifying fewer ideas yet
costing more than twice as much QWK. That is consistent with coverage being the
one input monotone in the grade, its corruption moving a prediction along the
scale rather than off it, and with the decomposition representations carrying
what separates neighbouring grades; separating the two accounts would need the
error distribution we do not report.

The three mechanisms inside the decomposition are not interchangeable; each
removal degrades a different part of the prediction.
\textbf{Collapsing semantic units} from eight to one costs on Accuracy, Macro-F1
and MAE while QWK barely moves ($0.8591$ against $0.8958$): one unit is enough
to rank the ideas, not to place them on the right grade.
\textbf{Disabling slot competition}, which removes the softmax through which the
units compete for each token, reverses that --- QWK falls while MAE stays where
collapsing the units left it --- as one would expect if the units stopped
dividing the target between them, though the ablation shows the cost, not the
collapse.
\textbf{Removing noisy-OR support} costs the most of the three on all four
metrics and most of all on the rank-sensitive ones, which is what aggregating
per-prior support into one monotone score is for.

We also sweep the three parameters that govern how much the model is given (the
evidence budget $K$, the training fraction and the encoder input length) one at
a time from the default configuration of Table~\ref{tab:main}, in all three
domains. The largest moves Accuracy by at most $0.075$, against the $0.34$ that
separates our row from the best prompted row there, so none of them is what the
main comparison turns on; Appendix~\ref{app:sensitivity} reports the sweeps and
what they do and do not license.

The ablations show that corrupting the evidence costs Accuracy, but not what
the intact pipeline does with it: what the support matrix weights when every
retrieved prior is on topic, and whether the residual representation contains
linearly accessible grade information. Figure~\ref{fig:case-study} examines both
on three correctly predicted test targets. Panel (a) reports
the support $A_k$ and the joint coverage $C_J$ the trained model produces for
each target, read off the forward pass with nothing fit. Panels (b) and (c) and
the probe reported with them hold the three cases out and fit every supervised
step on $80\%$ of the remaining test ideas, scoring out of fold
(Appendix~\ref{app:case-study}).

Support follows the annotation rather than the retrieval. The \emph{large cover}
prior of the low case takes $0.838$ of the support; in the high case, whose five
priors were all retrieved on the target's own vocabulary and are therefore
equally plausible to the retriever, no support exceeds $0.007$, with the
moderate case between the two on its lone \emph{partial cover} prior. Joint
coverage and the mean unit residual move monotonically across the three cases in
opposite directions. The residual $\boldsymbol{z}_R$ carries this linearly: a
probe on $\boldsymbol{z}_R$ alone reaches $0.730$ Accuracy
against a majority baseline of $0.329$, and the held-out ideas lie along the
first discriminant axis in grade order. The probe remains below the full model, so the grades are linearly
accessible without being linearly separable or the prediction heads redundant.
