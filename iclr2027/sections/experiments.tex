\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

The evaluation set is the \textsc{IdeaDelta} test split. Every system receives
the same evidence --- the
five highest-ranked admissible priors per target, as titles and abstracts, with
no paper text --- and is scored against the same labels by the same script, so a
difference between two rows is a difference in how the evidence is used. On a four-level label with unequal support, accuracy rewards the
majority class and MAE the middle of the scale, while quadratic weighted kappa
is what a constant predictor cannot win, so we report all four.
Appendix~\ref{app:setup} gives the splits, hyperparameters and scoring code.

We compare against a \emph{direct} single call, the \emph{RINoBench}
\citep{schopf2026rinobench} and \emph{UKPLab 3-stage} \citep{afzal2026beyond}
prompt chains, \emph{GraphMind} \citep{dasilva2025graphmind}, which labels each
prior as supporting or contrasting before rating, and \emph{DeepReviewer-7B}
\citep{zhu2025deepreview}, a reviewer model fine-tuned on paper review. The
first four appear once per backbone. Table~\ref{tab:panel-a} reports every
system on the \textsc{IdeaDelta} evaluation set, and Table~\ref{tab:panel-b}
carries the same frozen checkpoint to RINoBench. In the first table the prompted
systems occupy a band well below our row.

Among the prompted systems, adding stages to the prompt does not add
information, and on a strong backbone it removes some. Measured against a
single five-prior call on the same backbone, the two-stage RINoBench chain
gains in all three domains on Qwen3.5, by $0.024$ to $0.053$ accuracy and
$0.016$ to $0.086$ kappa; on deepseek-flash the gain is spent, the chain
staying within $0.026$ accuracy and $0.020$ kappa of the direct call in either
direction; and on gpt-5.6-luna it loses in all three domains, by $0.027$ to
$0.053$ accuracy and $0.064$ to $0.103$ kappa. The three-stage UKPLab chain and
GraphMind never reach the direct call at all: each sits below it in all nine
cells on both metrics, and the UKPLab deficit widens with the backbone in the
same direction, averaging $0.19$ kappa on Qwen3.5, $0.26$ on deepseek-flash and
$0.28$ on gpt-5.6-luna. A chain compensates for a weak reader, and once the
reader is strong the compensation costs more than it returns --- and it is
charged for twice, since GraphMind spends $476$ seconds and $31{,}201$ LLM
calls on the $3{,}352$ ideas to arrive below one call per idea.

Which model a chain runs on matters about as much as which chain it is. Holding
the chain fixed and changing the model moves accuracy by $0.107$ on average,
against $0.067$ for holding the model fixed and changing the chain; on kappa the
two swap, $0.204$ against $0.263$. Neither factor dominates the other, and which
of them leads depends on the metric read.

EGRD outperforms the baselines in all three domains.
Its accuracy margin over the best baseline in each domain is
$0.356$ on Graph ML, $0.341$ on computer vision, and $0.344$
on time series; the corresponding QWK margins are
$0.330$, $0.321$, and $0.322$.
These results show that the advantage is consistent across
the three evaluated domains, although individual baselines
remain sensitive to the domain.

Fine-tuning on paper review does not transfer to ideas.
DeepReviewer-7B is the one system trained rather than prompted, and its kappa
of $0.034$, $-0.043$ and $-0.017$ says it does not order the ideas. A model
trained to review whole papers appears to read a longer related-work block as
evidence of less novelty largely independently of what that work says, which is
what scoring a target against a retrieved set read as a block permits.

We evaluate the frozen checkpoint on RINoBench \citep{schopf2026rinobench},
whose 277 test ideas carry expert 1--5 labels. We fold them to four levels by
merging 4 and 5, which leaves $41.5\%$ of the ideas in the top class, so a
constant predictor scores $0.4152$ accuracy.

The ordering transfers even though the level does not.
EGRD leads Macro-F1 and kappa at $0.3407$ and $0.2245$ against $0.2457$ and
$0.1469$ for the best other system on each, and is the only row above $0.15$ kappa, while six of the eleven baselines sit
below $0.05$ and so do not order the ideas at all. Accuracy carries no
information here: our row reaches the constant predictor's
$0.4152$ with a prediction that is not constant, $16/36/76/149$ ideas across
the four classes against a gold $15/60/87/115$. The margin is modest: we read it as some agreement between residual structure
learned on model-generated labels and expert judgment, not as the problem
solved.

Two properties of the transfer bound what that agreement can be, and both are
visible before any model is run. The first is coverage. \textsc{IdeaDelta} is
built from three fields, whereas RINoBench draws its ideas from OpenReview
submissions across machine learning as a whole, so most of its test ideas sit in
areas --- robust reinforcement learning, tabular learning, computational biology
--- whose prior work the model was never trained to read; only about $30$ of the
$277$ ideas fall in the three fields it was. The second is granularity. Merging
the top two expert levels is what collapses $41.5\%$ of the ideas into one class,
and it also erases a distinction the experts themselves chose to draw, so a model
that orders the ideas correctly still has no way to express the difference
between a 4 and a 5.

Because the evidence is supplied rather than retrieved, it can be intervened on
with the target held fixed, which separates a model that reads the evidence
from one that recognises gradable phrasing.

Every stage of the pipeline is load-bearing, and error accumulates along it.
The first three interventions cut the chain at successive points.
\textbf{Permuting prior evidence} shuffles the retrieved prior texts across
targets, destroying the semantic content the chain is built on;
\textbf{permuting coverage} shuffles only the joint coverage score and leaves
every prior text in place, destroying what the historical evidence inputs
(\S\ref{sec:method-evidence}) infer from that content; \textbf{zeroing
residuals} sets $\boldsymbol{z}_U$, $\boldsymbol{z}_I$ and $\boldsymbol{z}_C$ to
zero, destroying what the evidence-grounded residual decomposition
(\S\ref{sec:method-decomposition}) adds on top of both. The ablations show that
all three are indispensable.
Permuting prior evidence costs $0.43$ accuracy and $0.67$ kappa, dropping
accuracy into the
band of the majority-class predictor of Table~\ref{tab:panel-a}
($0.312$--$0.356$): the grade is not recoverable from the target alone, so the
priors are read rather than treated as a pretext for grading the target's own
phrasing. Permuting coverage costs $0.11$ accuracy and $0.30$ kappa
with the texts intact, so what the historical evidence inputs infer is not
redundant with the evidence it is computed from. Zeroing residuals
costs $0.14$ accuracy, $0.12$ Macro-F1 and $0.11$ kappa, so the residual
decomposition is not an ornament on a prediction the evidence inputs already
support. Taken in order, the damage grows the earlier the cut falls: what one
stage corrupts it passes on, and the corruption is amplified rather than
absorbed downstream. Accuracy alone
reverses at the last step, $0.14$ for zeroing residuals against $0.11$
for permuting coverage, and it reverses for a reason: the residual
decomposition moves the metric that counts exact grades far more than the one
that counts agreement in rank, so what it contributes is which grade an idea
receives rather than how the ideas are ordered.

The mechanisms inside the residual decomposition earn their place, and two of
the three act on the ordering. The remaining interventions leave the pipeline
intact and remove one mechanism from the decomposition itself.
\textbf{Collapsing semantic units}, from eight to one, costs $0.12$ accuracy,
$0.15$ Macro-F1 and $0.16$
High-F1 but only $0.04$ kappa, so the collapse loses the grade and
leaves the ordering nearly intact. \textbf{Disabling slot competition}, which
removes the softmax through which the units compete for each token, costs
$0.11$ accuracy and $0.13$ kappa, reversing that pattern: deciding which unit a
token belongs to is what keeps the units apart in the first place.
\textbf{Removing noisy-OR support} costs the most of the three on every metric,
$0.16$ accuracy, $0.23$ Macro-F1, $0.27$ kappa and $0.20$ High-F1, so
aggregating support across priors is what makes the ordering work. For the
collapsed units and the removed noisy-OR, High-F1 falls further than accuracy,
which is where a decomposition should matter: the top grade turns on parts of a
target that no single prior supports.

We also sweep the three parameters that govern how much the model is given (the
evidence budget $K$, the training fraction and the encoder input length) one at
a time from the default configuration of Table~\ref{tab:panel-a}, in all three
domains. The largest of the three moves accuracy by at most $0.075$, against the
$0.34$ that separates our row from the best prompted row of
Table~\ref{tab:panel-a}, so none of them is what the main comparison turns on.
The sweeps, and what they do and do not license, are in
Appendix~\ref{app:sensitivity}.
