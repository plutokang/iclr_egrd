\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

The evaluation set is the \textsc{IdeaDelta} test split. Every system receives
the same evidence --- the
five highest-ranked admissible priors per target, as titles and abstracts, with
no paper text --- and is scored against the same labels by the same script.
For the four-level labels with unequal class support, we report Accuracy,
Macro-F1, QWK and MAE to assess exact classification, performance across
classes, chance-corrected ordinal agreement and grade error, respectively.
Appendix~\ref{app:setup} gives the splits, hyperparameters and scoring code.

We compare against a \emph{direct} single call, the \emph{RINoBench}
\citep{schopf2026rinobench} and \emph{UKPLab 3-stage} \citep{afzal2026beyond}
prompt chains, \emph{GraphMind} \citep{dasilva2025graphmind}, which labels each
prior as supporting or contrasting before rating, and \emph{DeepReviewer-7B}
\citep{zhu2025deepreview}, a reviewer model fine-tuned on paper review. The
first four appear once per backbone. Table~\ref{tab:panel-a} reports every
system on the \textsc{IdeaDelta} evaluation set, and Table~\ref{tab:panel-b}
carries the same frozen checkpoint to RINoBench. In the first table the prompted
systems occupy a band well below our row.

The benefit of multi-stage prompting depends on the chain and backbone;
adding stages does not consistently improve performance. Measured against a
single five-prior call on the same backbone, the two-stage RINoBench chain
gains in all three domains on Qwen3.5, by $0.024$ to $0.053$ Accuracy and
$0.016$ to $0.086$ QWK; on deepseek-flash the chain stays
within $0.026$ Accuracy and $0.020$ QWK of the direct call in either
direction; and on gpt-5.6-luna it loses in all three domains, by $0.027$ to
$0.053$ Accuracy and $0.064$ to $0.103$ QWK. The three-stage UKPLab chain and
GraphMind never reach the direct call at all: each sits below it in all nine
cells on both metrics, and the UKPLab deficit widens with the backbone in the
same direction, averaging $0.19$ QWK on Qwen3.5, $0.26$ on deepseek-flash and
$0.28$ on gpt-5.6-luna. These results show no consistent advantage from
additional prompting stages. GraphMind uses $476$ seconds and $31{,}201$ LLM
calls for the $3{,}352$ ideas, yet scores below the direct single-call baseline.

Which model a chain runs on matters about as much as which chain it is. Holding
the chain fixed and changing the model moves Accuracy by $0.107$ on average,
against $0.067$ for holding the model fixed and changing the chain; on QWK the
two swap, $0.204$ against $0.263$. Neither factor dominates the other, and which
of them leads depends on the metric read.

EGRD outperforms the baselines in all three domains.
Its Accuracy margin over the best baseline in each domain is
$0.356$ on Graph ML, $0.341$ on computer vision, and $0.344$
on time series; the corresponding QWK margins are
$0.330$, $0.321$, and $0.322$.
These results show that the advantage is consistent across
the three evaluated domains, although individual baselines
remain sensitive to the domain.

DeepReviewer-7B shows limited transfer to this idea-level evaluation.
Its QWK values of $0.034$, $-0.043$ and $-0.017$ indicate ordinal agreement
close to chance across the three domains under the supplied-evidence protocol.

We evaluate the frozen checkpoint on RINoBench \citep{schopf2026rinobench},
whose 277 test ideas carry expert 1--5 labels. We fold them to four levels by
merging 4 and 5, which leaves $41.5\%$ of the ideas in the top class, so a
a predictor that always selects the top class scores $0.4152$ Accuracy.

Transfer to expert labels yields modest ordinal agreement.
EGRD leads Macro-F1 and QWK at $0.3407$ and $0.2245$ against $0.2457$ and
$0.1469$ for the best other system on each, and is the only row above $0.15$ QWK, while six of the eleven baselines sit
below $0.05$ QWK. Accuracy alone is insufficient to assess performance here:
our row reaches the majority-class predictor's
$0.4152$ with a prediction that is not constant, $16/36/76/149$ ideas across
the four classes against a gold $15/60/87/115$. The margin is modest: we read it as some agreement between residual structure
learned on model-generated labels and expert judgment, not as the problem
solved.

Two properties of the transfer bound what that agreement can be, and both are
visible before any model is run. The first is coverage. \textsc{IdeaDelta} is
built from three fields, whereas RINoBench draws its ideas from OpenReview
submissions across machine learning as a whole, so most of its test ideas sit in
areas --- robust reinforcement learning, tabular learning, computational biology
--- whose prior work the model was never trained to read; only about $30$ of the
$277$ ideas fall in the three fields it was. The second is granularity. Merging
the top two expert levels is what collapses $41.5\%$ of the ideas into one class,
and it also erases a distinction the experts themselves chose to draw, so a model
that orders the ideas correctly still has no way to express the difference
between a 4 and a 5.

The supplied evidence can be intervened on with the target held fixed,
allowing us to test whether the trained model's predictions depend on evidence
matched to the target.

Each of the three pipeline interventions reduces performance.
The first three interventions cut the chain at successive points.
\textbf{Permuting prior evidence} shuffles the retrieved prior texts across
targets, destroying the semantic content the chain is built on;
\textbf{permuting coverage} shuffles only the joint coverage score and leaves
every prior text in place, destroying what the historical evidence inputs
(\S\ref{sec:method-evidence}) infer from that content; \textbf{zeroing
decomposition representations} sets the unit residual $\boldsymbol{z}_U$,
interaction residual $\boldsymbol{z}_I$ and covered-content representation
$\boldsymbol{z}_C$ to zero, removing these three outputs of the decomposition
(\S\ref{sec:method-decomposition}) together. The ablations show that
all three are indispensable.
Permuting prior evidence costs $0.43$ Accuracy and $0.67$ QWK, dropping
Accuracy into the
band of the majority-class predictor of Table~\ref{tab:panel-a}
($0.312$--$0.356$). This drop shows that the trained model relies on evidence
matched to the target. Permuting coverage costs $0.11$ Accuracy and $0.30$ QWK
with the texts intact, so what the historical evidence inputs infer is not
redundant with the evidence it is computed from. Zeroing the decomposition
representations costs $0.14$ Accuracy, $0.12$ Macro-F1 and $0.11$ QWK,
showing that the trained model benefits from these representations collectively.
Permuting prior evidence causes the largest performance drop, while
perturbing coverage and zeroing the decomposition representations also reduce performance.
The latter causes a larger drop than permuting coverage in both
Accuracy ($0.1425$ versus $0.1147$) and Macro-F1 ($0.1236$ versus $0.1185$),
with a smaller difference in Macro-F1.

The three decomposition-design ablations reduce performance, with different
effects across metrics. These interventions leave the pipeline
intact and remove one mechanism from the decomposition itself.
\textbf{Collapsing semantic units}, from eight to one, costs $0.12$ Accuracy,
$0.15$ Macro-F1, while QWK decreases by $0.04$ and remains at $0.8591$.
\textbf{Disabling slot competition}, which
removes the softmax through which the units compete for each token, costs
$0.11$ Accuracy and $0.13$ QWK, reversing that pattern: deciding which unit a
token belongs to is what keeps the units apart in the first place.
\textbf{Removing noisy-OR support} causes the largest decreases of the three
in Accuracy, Macro-F1 and QWK, by $0.16$, $0.23$ and $0.27$, respectively,
supporting the contribution of this aggregation rule to ordinal agreement.

We also sweep the three parameters that govern how much the model is given (the
evidence budget $K$, the training fraction and the encoder input length) one at
a time from the default configuration of Table~\ref{tab:panel-a}, in all three
domains. The largest of the three moves Accuracy by at most $0.075$, against the
$0.34$ that separates our row from the best prompted row of
Table~\ref{tab:panel-a}, so none of them is what the main comparison turns on.
The sweeps, and what they do and do not license, are in
Appendix~\ref{app:sensitivity}.

The ablations show that corrupting the evidence costs Accuracy, not what the
intact pipeline does with it: what the support matrix weights when every
retrieved prior is on topic, and whether the residual representation contains
linearly accessible grade information. Figure~\ref{fig:case-study} examines
these questions using three correctly predicted
test targets, and is built as follows. Panel (a) reports the support $A_k$ and
the joint coverage $C_J$ that the trained model produces for each target; these
are read off the forward pass, and nothing in the panel is fit here. For the
other two, the test ideas are split $80/20$ with the three cases held out, and
every supervised step is fit on the $80\%$ part alone. Panel (b) standardises
$\boldsymbol{z}_R$ on that part, ranks its coordinates by univariate
$F$-statistic against the grade, and shows the $140$ that separate it best.
Panel (c) reduces $\boldsymbol{z}_R$ to $50$ principal components and projects
it onto the first two axes of a shrinkage linear discriminant fit on the four
grades. The probe reported there is separate from both: a five-fold
cross-validated multinomial logistic regression on standardised
$\boldsymbol{z}_R$, scored out of fold. Target and prior texts are in
Appendix~\ref{app:case-study}.

Support follows the annotation rather than the retrieval: the \emph{large cover}
prior of the low case takes $0.838$, the lone \emph{partial cover} of the
moderate case $0.523$, and in the high case, whose five priors were all
retrieved on the target's own vocabulary, no support exceeds $0.007$. Joint
coverage follows at $0.62$, $0.32$ and $0.12$, and so does the mean unit
residual, at $0.041$, $0.408$ and $0.977$. The representation
$\boldsymbol{z}_R$ contains substantial linearly accessible grade information:
a probe using only $\boldsymbol{z}_R$ achieves $0.730$ Accuracy against a majority
baseline of $0.329$, and the held-out ideas lie along the first discriminant
axis in grade order. The probe remains below the full model; its performance
shows that grade information is accessible to a linear classifier, without
establishing complete linear separability or making the final prediction heads
redundant.
