\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

The evaluation set is the \textsc{IdeaDelta} test split. Every system is scored
against the same labels by the same script, and all but one receive the same
evidence --- the five highest-ranked admissible priors per target, as titles and
abstracts, with no paper text. The exception is a dense-retrieval baseline that
assembles its own five priors at query time, included to measure what the
supplied evidence contributes. For the four-level labels with unequal class
support, we report Accuracy, Macro-F1, QWK and MAE --- exact classification,
performance across classes, chance-corrected ordinal agreement and grade error.
Appendix~\ref{app:setup} gives the splits, hyperparameters and scoring code.

We compare against a \emph{direct} single call; two multi-stage prompt chains,
\emph{Schopf 2-stage}, the evaluation prompt accompanying RINoBench
\citep{schopf2026rinobench}, and \emph{UKPLab 3-stage}
\citep{afzal2026beyond}; \emph{GraphMind} \citep{dasilva2025graphmind}, which
labels each prior as supporting or contrasting before rating;
\emph{DeepReviewer-7B} \citep{zhu2025deepreview}, a reviewer model fine-tuned on
paper review; and a \emph{dense RAG} group running the \emph{direct} call on
five priors it retrieves itself by dense similarity to the target. All but
DeepReviewer-7B appear once per backbone. Table~\ref{tab:panel-a} reports every
system on the \textsc{IdeaDelta} evaluation set, and Table~\ref{tab:panel-b}
carries the same frozen checkpoint to RINoBench.

The benefit of multi-stage prompting depends on the chain and the backbone.
Measured against a single five-prior call on the same backbone, the two-stage
Schopf chain gains in all three domains on Qwen3.5, by $0.024$ to $0.053$
Accuracy and $0.016$ to $0.086$ QWK; on deepseek-flash it stays within $0.026$
Accuracy and $0.020$ QWK of the direct call in either direction; and on
gpt-5.6-luna it loses in all three domains, by $0.027$ to $0.053$ Accuracy and
$0.064$ to $0.103$ QWK. The three-stage UKPLab chain and GraphMind never reach
the direct call at all, sitting below it in all nine cells on both metrics; the
UKPLab QWK deficit widens with the backbone in the same direction, averaging
$0.19$ on Qwen3.5, $0.26$ on deepseek-flash and $0.28$ on gpt-5.6-luna.
Additional stages therefore buy no consistent advantage: GraphMind spends $476$
seconds and $31{,}201$ LLM calls on the $3{,}352$ ideas and still scores below
the direct single call.

Which model a chain runs on matters about as much as which chain it is. Over
the four chains that share the supplied evidence, holding the chain fixed and
changing the model moves Accuracy by $0.103$ on average --- the mean, across
the four chains and three domains, of the range over the three backbones ---
against $0.091$ for the mean range over the four chains at a fixed backbone;
on QWK the two swap, $0.195$ against $0.348$. Neither factor dominates on both
metrics, and which of them leads depends on the metric read.

Replacing the supplied priors with query-time dense retrieval costs ordinal
agreement on every backbone. Against the direct call on the same backbone and
domain, Macro-F1 and QWK each fall in all nine cells, QWK by $0.109$
to $0.190$ on Qwen3.5, $0.355$ to $0.406$ on deepseek-flash and $0.284$ to
$0.326$ on gpt-5.6-luna. Accuracy moves the other way on the weakest backbone,
rising by $0.027$ to $0.060$ on Qwen3.5 while Macro-F1 falls, the
signature of predictions concentrating on the larger classes: these
rows sit at $0.318$--$0.372$ Accuracy, at or just above the majority-class
band, with Macro-F1 no higher than $0.295$ anywhere. The group also
compresses the backbone differences that Table~\ref{tab:panel-a} otherwise
shows, its Accuracy spanning at most $0.028$ in any
domain against $0.112$ to $0.154$ for the direct call. What a system is given to
compare against therefore moves the judgment more than which model or chain
performs it, which sharpens the evidence check of
Appendix~\ref{app:dataset-validity}: there random priors reduce label
prediction to near-chance and establish only that the evidence must be on
topic, whereas dense retrieval is on topic and the gap persists, so the
candidate lists carry target--prior relationships that generic similarity
search does not recover --- what the cross-type pass and the adjudicated
similarity band (\S\ref{sec:dataset}) are for.

EGRD outperforms the baselines in all three domains, by
$0.356$, $0.341$ and $0.344$ Accuracy and $0.330$, $0.321$ and $0.322$ QWK over
the best baseline of each domain, so the advantage is consistent across the
three even though individual baselines remain sensitive to the domain.
DeepReviewer-7B transfers least: its QWK of $0.034$, $-0.043$ and $-0.017$ is
ordinal agreement close to chance under the supplied-evidence protocol.

We evaluate the frozen checkpoint on RINoBench \citep{schopf2026rinobench},
whose 277 test ideas carry expert 1--5 labels, folding them to four levels by
merging 4 and 5; that leaves $41.5\%$ of the ideas in the top class, so a
predictor that always selects it scores $0.4152$ Accuracy.
Transfer to those labels yields modest ordinal agreement. EGRD leads Macro-F1
and QWK at $0.3407$ and $0.2245$ against $0.2457$ and $0.1469$ for the best
other system on each, and is the only row above $0.15$ QWK, while six of the
eleven baselines sit below $0.05$. Accuracy alone is insufficient here: our row
reaches the majority-class predictor's $0.4152$ with a prediction that is not
constant, $16/36/76/149$ ideas across the four classes against a gold
$15/60/87/115$. We read the margin as some agreement between residual structure
learned on model-generated labels and expert judgment, not as the problem
solved.

Two properties of the transfer bound what that agreement can be, and both are
visible before any model is run. \textsc{IdeaDelta} is built from three fields,
whereas RINoBench draws on OpenReview submissions across machine learning as a
whole, so only about $30$ of its $277$ test ideas fall in the fields whose prior
work the model was trained to read. And merging the top two expert levels, which
is what collapses $41.5\%$ of the ideas into one class, erases a distinction the
experts chose to draw, leaving a correctly ordering model no way to express the
difference between a 4 and a 5.

Because the supplied evidence can be intervened on with the target held fixed,
we can test what the trained model's predictions actually depend on. The three
pipeline interventions cut the chain at successive points.
\textbf{Permuting prior evidence} shuffles the retrieved prior texts across
targets, destroying the semantic content the chain is built on;
\textbf{permuting coverage} shuffles only the joint coverage score and leaves
every prior text in place, destroying what the historical evidence inputs
(\S\ref{sec:method-evidence}) infer from that content; \textbf{zeroing
decomposition representations} sets the unit residual $\boldsymbol{z}_U$,
interaction residual $\boldsymbol{z}_I$ and covered-content representation
$\boldsymbol{z}_C$ to zero, removing these three outputs of the decomposition
(\S\ref{sec:method-decomposition}) together. All three reduce performance on
every metric.
Permuting prior evidence costs the most, $0.43$ Accuracy and $0.67$ QWK, and
raises MAE from $0.2230$ to $0.8606$, dropping Accuracy into the
band of the majority-class predictor of Table~\ref{tab:panel-a}
($0.312$--$0.356$). This drop shows that the trained model relies on evidence
matched to the target. Permuting coverage leaves every prior text in place and
still costs $0.11$ Accuracy, $0.30$ QWK and $0.35$ MAE, so what the historical
evidence inputs infer is not redundant with the evidence it is computed from.
Zeroing the decomposition representations costs $0.14$ Accuracy, $0.12$
Macro-F1, $0.11$ QWK and $0.21$ MAE, showing that the trained model benefits
from these representations collectively. These last two interventions order
differently under the two kinds of metric: zeroing costs more in Accuracy
($0.1425$ versus $0.1147$) and Macro-F1 ($0.1236$ versus $0.1185$), permuting
coverage more in QWK ($0.2962$ versus $0.1080$) and MAE ($0.3472$ versus
$0.2136$), so corrupting the coverage signal damages the ordering of the grades
more even though it misclassifies fewer ideas.

The three decomposition-design ablations reduce performance, with different
effects across metrics. These interventions leave the pipeline
intact and remove one mechanism from the decomposition itself.
\textbf{Collapsing semantic units}, from eight to one, costs $0.12$ Accuracy,
$0.15$ Macro-F1 and $0.18$ MAE, while QWK decreases by $0.04$ and remains at
$0.8591$: a single unit still orders the ideas, but the grade it assigns is
off by more.
\textbf{Disabling slot competition}, which
removes the softmax through which the units compete for each token, costs
$0.11$ Accuracy and $0.13$ QWK at a comparable MAE ($0.3985$ against
$0.4073$), reversing that pattern: deciding which unit a
token belongs to is what keeps the units apart in the first place.
\textbf{Removing noisy-OR support} costs the most of the three on all four
metrics, $0.16$ Accuracy, $0.23$ Macro-F1, $0.27$ QWK and $0.38$ MAE,
supporting the contribution of this aggregation rule to ordinal agreement.

We also sweep the three parameters that govern how much the model is given (the
evidence budget $K$, the training fraction and the encoder input length) one at
a time from the default configuration of Table~\ref{tab:panel-a}, in all three
domains. The largest of the three moves Accuracy by at most $0.075$, against the
$0.34$ that separates our row from the best prompted row of
Table~\ref{tab:panel-a}, so none of them is what the main comparison turns on.
The sweeps, and what they do and do not license, are in
Appendix~\ref{app:sensitivity}.

The ablations show that corrupting the evidence costs Accuracy, not what the
intact pipeline does with it: what the support matrix weights when every
retrieved prior is on topic, and whether the residual representation contains
linearly accessible grade information. Figure~\ref{fig:case-study} examines
these questions on three correctly predicted test targets. Panel (a) reports
the support $A_k$ and the joint coverage $C_J$ the trained model produces for
each target, read off the forward pass with nothing fit. Panels (b) and (c) and
the probe reported with them hold the three cases out and fit every supervised
step on $80\%$ of the remaining test ideas, scoring out of fold.
Appendix~\ref{app:case-study} gives the construction of each panel and the
target and prior texts.

Support follows the annotation rather than the retrieval: the \emph{large cover}
prior of the low case takes $0.838$, the lone \emph{partial cover} of the
moderate case $0.523$, and in the high case, whose five priors were all
retrieved on the target's own vocabulary, no support exceeds $0.007$. Joint
coverage follows at $0.62$, $0.32$ and $0.12$, and so does the mean unit
residual, at $0.041$, $0.408$ and $0.977$. The representation
$\boldsymbol{z}_R$ contains substantial linearly accessible grade information:
a probe using only $\boldsymbol{z}_R$ achieves $0.730$ Accuracy against a majority
baseline of $0.329$, and the held-out ideas lie along the first discriminant
axis in grade order. The probe remains below the full model, so the grades are
linearly accessible without being linearly separable or the prediction heads
redundant.
