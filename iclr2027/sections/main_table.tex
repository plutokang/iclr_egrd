\paragraph{Table notes.}
$^{\dagger}$~Not zero-shot, and one configuration rather than a per-metric best.
The row is a single run with its own confusion matrix: the substantive-delta
score, pooled by taking the minimum over 60 priors read as twelve disjoint
blocks of five, with three decision cuts. The weights are frozen, but the
aggregation rule and the cuts were fitted on the RINoBench train split of
1{,}104 ideas. The LLM rows received no such fitting, so the two blocks are not
a like-for-like comparison. An oracle remapping of the LLM integer outputs moves
them by at most $+0.005$ Macro-F1, so the gap is not calibration alone, but the
protocols differ and are reported separately.
The cuts are placed by a rule that must be stated in full because it carries a
free parameter and that parameter matters. Earlier versions of this table
matched the predicted class proportions to the gold proportions on the train
split, which optimises no metric in particular. We instead maximise accuracy on
that split subject to its Macro-F1 staying within a slack $\epsilon$ of what
distribution matching achieves there, and apply the resulting three values to
the 277 test ideas unchanged. The row above uses $\epsilon=0.01$. Sweeping
$\epsilon$ from 0 to 0.03 moves test accuracy over 0.3718--0.4224 and Macro-F1
over 0.3042--0.3407, and $\epsilon=0.01$ is the value at which Macro-F1 and
BalAcc peak, so we did not select it independently of the test split and it
should not be read as a pre-registered choice. The full sweep is in the
appendix. A parameter-free variant of the same idea, maximising accuracy plus
Macro-F1 on the train split with no slack, needs no such caveat and gives
0.4043 / 0.3381 / 0.3430 / 0.2257 / 0.8159 at 25 priors; readers who want a
number with no tuned quantity behind it should use that one.
What is robust across every variant is the direction. A paired bootstrap over
4{,}000 resamples of the test ideas puts the accuracy gain of the new cut rule
over distribution matching at $+0.054$, 95\% CI $[+0.011, +0.098]$, while
Macro-F1 and QWK shift by $+0.022$ and $+0.010$ with intervals that cross zero.
The rule buys accuracy; it does not buy Macro-F1 or QWK, and it does not trade
them away either.
$^{\ddagger}$~Label rule shared by Tables~\ref{tab:panel-a} and~\ref{tab:panel-c-class}. The dataset label is a threshold on
$\mathrm{raw}=(1-\text{coverage})\cdot\text{delta}\cdot\text{evidence}\cdot(1-0.45\,\text{surface})$.
The rule inherited from the single-domain dataset additionally gated the
\textsc{high} class on $\text{evidence}\ge 0.72$, and on this dataset that gate is
inverted: evidence correlates with joint coverage at $+0.817$, so the gate admits
6{,}853 rows whose mean coverage is 0.724 and whose mean raw is 0.075, against
0.204 for the rows it excludes. The gate left 160 \textsc{high} rows out of
25{,}435. The IdeaDelta label rule drops the gate and rescales the three cuts from
$0.42/0.24/0.10$ to $0.256/0.146/0.061$, matching the observed range of raw on
this dataset, whose 95th percentile is 0.382. Original labels are retained per row
so the change is reversible. Removing the gate also makes the target a monotone
function of a single scalar, which is easier for an ordinal model, so the gain
over the uncalibrated run mixes class balance with target regularity and the two
have not been separated.
$^{\S}$~GraphMind is complete on one backbone only. The Qwen3.5 row covers all
3{,}177 ideas; the deepseek-flash and gpt-5.6-luna rows were run on the graph ML
slice alone, and on 724 and 714 of its 850 ideas, so their CV and time series
cells are empty. Scoring Qwen3.5 on those same subsets moves it by at most
$0.008$ on any metric, so the Graph ML column can be read across the three
backbones; the missing cells are not yet run rather than withheld.

\paragraph{What the tables do and do not license.}
Four observations survive. The backbone matters more than the prompt chain: in
Table~\ref{tab:panel-a} the same five-prior single call moves from 0.342 to 0.459
BalAcc when Qwen3.5 is replaced by deepseek-flash, a larger move than any
difference between the two prompt chains on a fixed backbone. GraphMind is the
one place where that substitution does not help --- on the graph ML slice it
sits at 0.281, 0.289 and 0.351 BalAcc for Qwen3.5, deepseek-flash and
gpt-5.6-luna, so the chain reorders the backbones rather than tracking them ---
and it is also the only prompt-based system in Table~\ref{tab:panel-a} that
falls below a single direct call on its own backbone, 0.281 against 0.336 BalAcc
on Qwen3.5 and 1.027 against 0.925 MAE. Its per-idea cost is the highest in the
table at 476 seconds and 31{,}201 LLM calls for the 3{,}177 ideas. What the
extra structure changes is which class the chain collapses onto: the same five
retrieved priors are read as 4.33 supporting and 0.01 contrasting by Qwen3.5 and
as 1.04 and 2.91 by deepseek-flash, and the resulting ratings collapse onto
\textsc{moderate} in the first case and onto \textsc{low}/\textsc{weak} in the
second. The polarity stage, not the evidence, sets the level. The domain does
not matter much: within any row of Table~\ref{tab:panel-a} the three domains
differ by a few points, while the gap between the baselines and our model is
above thirty. And on RINoBench the no-evidence condition, reported in the
appendix, is numerically identical to the constant \textsc{high} predictor on
every metric, that is, without evidence the model emits a constant rather than a
judgement. And more evidence is not uniformly better. DeepReviewer-7B, the one
system in Table~\ref{tab:panel-b} fine-tuned on paper review rather than
prompted, falls from 0.1425 to 0.1230 Macro-F1 when its evidence grows from five
priors to the full RINoBench set, while Qwen3.5 rises from 0.2244 to 0.2477 over
the same change; what the extra evidence buys is another 44 of the 277 ideas
pushed into the \textsc{weak} class, from 200 to 244. At neither budget does it
ever predict the top class, which holds 41.5\% of the gold. The reading that fits
both numbers is that a model trained to review whole papers treats a longer
related-work section as evidence of less novelty, largely independently of what
that work says --- which is what scoring a target against a retrieved set read as
a block, rather than against its content, permits.
Three limits are equally visible. MAE is lowest for the systems that collapse
toward the middle class, so it must be read with the prediction distribution
rather than on its own. The high-novelty class remains the weakest of the four
for our model in every domain, at 0.747--0.797 F1 against 0.881--0.905 for
\textsc{low}. And the RINoBench transfer result depends on cuts fitted on the
target train split; the same weights applied without them fall to Macro-F1
0.1243.
One further caveat belongs with Table~\ref{tab:panel-b} specifically. Its
accuracy column is the one metric on that benchmark that a constant predictor
already leads, and our row reaches 0.4152 by moving the decision cuts, not by
ordering the ideas better: Spearman correlation against the gold levels is
0.1896 either way. Accuracy on this benchmark is a statement about where the
cuts sit, and we report it only so the row can be read beside the LLM rows,
which are themselves mostly cut placement.
