\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Language models now propose research ideas faster than anyone can vet them, and
deciding which of those ideas is new is the step that does not scale. Novelty is
not a monotone function of textual similarity to any single prior work. We
therefore study \emph{evidence-conditioned} novelty assessment: grading a target
idea against explicit prior works published before it, making judgments
inspectable and enabling evidence interventions.

Most prior work takes a paper as the unit of assessment, from citation-pattern
measures \citep{uzzi2013atypical} to document-level embeddings
\citep{cohan2020specter,ostendorff2022scincl} to systems that review a
submission as a whole \citep{zhu2025deepreview}. But a paper bundles
contributions of uneven novelty, and one score over the bundle does not say
which contribution it describes. Systems that do operate on ideas assemble the
judgment as an LLM workflow: retrieve related work, then
prompt an LLM to compare the target idea against it
\citep{wang2024scimon,dasilva2025graphmind,afzal2026beyond}. Here the evidence
is selected by the system being evaluated, so no verdict can be attributed to
the retrieval or to the comparison, and the comparison itself is elicited by a
prompt rather than learned against supervision. Evaluating this arrangement
against expert judgments, \citet{schopf2026rinobench} find that the reasoning
such models produce mirrors human rationales, yet that alignment does not
translate into accurate grades.

Even when the target idea and the available prior works are held fixed,
novelty assessment depends on how their relationship is represented. As
illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:motivation}(a), a prompt-based judge compares
the target with earlier publications, but leaves the correspondence between
individual parts of the target and their supporting evidence implicit. This
correspondence matters for three reasons. First, support can be \emph{dispersed}
across multiple papers, each accounting for a different part of the idea.
Second, similar overall coverage can leave substantively different content
unsupported, such as an implementation detail in one case and a central
mechanism in another. Third, support for individual components does not
necessarily establish support for their \emph{combination}. These distinctions
motivate an explicit representation of what the supplied evidence supports
and what remains unsupported.

We propose Evidence-Grounded Residual Decomposition (EGRD) for idea-level
novelty prediction. As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:motivation}(b), EGRD aligns latent semantic
units with supplied prior works and combines residuals for unsupported content
and potentially novel combinations with the evidence context to predict an
ordered novelty grade.

Because
existing benchmarks do not pair ideas with the evidence admissible when they
were proposed, we also construct \textsc{IdeaDelta} to supply that pairing. Our
contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{\textsc{IdeaDelta}, a multi-domain evidence-paired dataset.}
  $21{,}111$ ideas paired with temporally admissible priors and annotated at the
  idea and pair levels, with $92{,}876$ pairs across graph machine learning,
  computer vision, and time series. Supplied rather than retrieved, the evidence
  supports controlled interventions that isolate the effect of prior work on
  novelty judgments.
  \item \textbf{Unit-level coverage over dispersed evidence.} We model coverage
  at the level of latent semantic units, aligning each unit with each prior
  through a learned support matrix. Noisy-OR aggregation lets several partially
  relevant papers jointly cover a unit none covers alone.
  \item \textbf{Typed residuals.} We split what the priors leave over into unit
  residuals for content no prior supports and interaction residuals for
  combinations no single prior puts together. Both are derived from the same support matrix.
  \item \textbf{Consistent gains across domains.}
  EGRD consistently outperforms all evaluated baselines across all three
  domains. We further evaluate transfer to expert annotations on RINoBench
  \citep{schopf2026rinobench} using a frozen checkpoint.
\end{itemize}

Model and training code for all three stages is available for review in an
anonymized repository at
\url{https://anonymous.4open.science/r/EGRD_model-86D7}, and \textsc{IdeaDelta}
is available under an anonymized account at
\url{https://huggingface.co/datasets/holaholaIDEADELTA/IdeaDelta}.
