\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Language models now propose research ideas faster than anyone can vet them, and
deciding which of those ideas is new is the step that does not scale. Novelty is
not a monotone function of textual similarity to any single prior work. We
therefore study \emph{evidence-conditioned} novelty assessment: grading a target
idea against an explicit set of prior works published before it, which makes the
basis of each judgment inspectable and turns the evidence into a variable we can
intervene on.

Most prior work takes a paper as the unit of assessment, from citation-pattern
measures \citep{uzzi2013atypical} to document-level embeddings
\citep{cohan2020specter,ostendorff2022scincl} to systems that review a
submission as a whole \citep{zhu2025deepreview}. But a paper bundles
contributions of uneven novelty, and one score over the bundle does not say
which contribution it describes. Systems that do operate on ideas assemble the
judgment as a language-model workflow: retrieve related work, then prompt a
model to compare against it
\citep{wang2024scimon,dasilva2025graphmind,afzal2026beyond}. Here the evidence
is selected by the system being evaluated, so no verdict can be attributed to
the retrieval or to the comparison, and the comparison itself is elicited by a
prompt rather than learned against supervision. Evaluating this arrangement
against expert judgments, \citet{schopf2026rinobench} find that the reasoning
such models produce mirrors human rationales, yet that alignment does not
translate into accurate grades.

Such a workflow also compresses the comparison into a single score, and three
distinctions do not survive. Coverage can be \emph{dispersed}: several papers
may jointly account for an idea that no single paper resembles. Ideas at equal
coverage differ in \emph{what remains}: one leaves an implementation detail
unexplained, another introduces a new mechanism. And individually covered
components may form a \emph{combination} that no single prior work puts
together. Each distinction asks about a particular part of the idea and the
particular prior work responsible for it, and a score that discards that
correspondence cannot be post-processed to recover it. Assessing novelty calls
instead for a representation that keeps it.

We propose Evidence-Grounded Residual Decomposition (EGRD), which decomposes a
target against ranked historical evidence into what that evidence accounts for
and what it leaves, then predicts an ordered novelty grade from the two. Because
existing benchmarks do not pair ideas with the evidence admissible when they
were proposed, we also construct \textsc{IdeaDelta} to supply that pairing. Our
contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{\textsc{IdeaDelta}, a multi-domain evidence-paired dataset.}
  $21{,}111$ ideas paired with temporally admissible priors and annotated at the
  idea and pair levels ($92{,}876$ pairs) across graph machine learning,
  computer vision, and time series. Supplied rather than retrieved, the evidence
  can be intervened on: permuting priors while holding targets fixed drops
  accuracy from $0.78$ to $0.35$, the majority-class band.
  \item \textbf{Unit-level coverage over dispersed evidence.} We model coverage
  at the level of latent semantic units, aligning each unit with each prior
  through a learned support matrix. Noisy-OR aggregation lets several partially
  relevant papers jointly cover a unit none covers alone; removing it costs
  $0.27$ quadratic weighted kappa (QWK).
  \item \textbf{Typed residuals.} We split what the priors leave over into unit
  residuals (content no prior supports) and interaction residuals (combinations
  no single prior puts together). Both read off the same support matrix, so an
  unexplored contribution and an effective recombination are distinguished by
  the model rather than by assertion.
  \item \textbf{Consistent gains across domains and transfer to expert judgments.}
  EGRD reaches $0.909/0.882/0.898$ QWK across the three domains against
  $0.579$ for the best prompted system. A blockwise rule extends the frozen checkpoint
  beyond its training evidence budget, leading every RINoBench
  \citep{schopf2026rinobench} baseline on QWK ($0.2245$ against $0.1469$) and
  Macro-F1 ($0.3407$ against $0.2457$); that margin is modest.
\end{itemize}
