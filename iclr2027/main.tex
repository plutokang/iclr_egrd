\begin{abstract}
Language models now propose research ideas faster than anyone can vet them,
which makes assessing novelty a practical bottleneck. Judging novelty requires
identifying what prior work already covers and evaluating the contribution that
remains. Overall similarity provides an incomplete account: an idea's components
may be covered across several papers, while their combination may still
introduce a substantive change. We propose Evidence-Grounded Residual
Decomposition (EGRD), which assesses novelty relative to an explicit set of
temporally admissible prior works, making the basis of each judgment inspectable
and allowing the effect of changing the evidence to be examined. EGRD estimates
how much of the target those priors jointly cover, decomposes the target into
latent semantic units, and learns a support matrix aligning these units with the
evidence. It models two complementary residuals: unit residuals capture content
with limited historical support, while interaction residuals capture unit
combinations that no single prior supports. These residuals, together with
coverage and contextual representations, predict an ordered novelty grade. A
conservative blockwise aggregation rule lets a model trained with a small
evidence budget read larger evidence sets at inference time without retraining.
We further construct \textsc{IdeaDelta}, a dataset spanning graph machine
learning, computer vision, and time series, pairing each idea with temporally admissible priors annotated
for coverage, residual contribution, and novelty. EGRD recovers the annotation
scheme consistently across the three domains, and ablations show that its
predictions depend on the supplied evidence and on the residual representations
rather than on the target idea alone. Transfer to expert judgments remains
sensitive to where the grade boundaries are placed.
\end{abstract}
